## Prepared text

Dear Professor Felicity Scott,

Your profile links Outlaw Territories to environments of insecurity and architectural governance (https://www.arch.columbia.edu/faculty/29-felicity-scott). That raises a precise question for the study: where does authorization acquire a physical boundary?

I’m developing Proxy Society, an architecture research project on AI agents and robots acting for absent people under limited, revocable mandates. I would compare a staffed library entrance with a gated proxy handoff recess: the same limited instruction and expiry, but different paths to question a refusal, revoke the instruction and enter personally. The plans would test how an apparently convenient service becomes spatial governance.

Could you advise whether this belongs in an architecture history/theory dissertation, and which historical counterexample would challenge the premise before developing prototypes?

Best regards,
Kris Haamer
Proxy Society
https://proxysociety.org/architecture
